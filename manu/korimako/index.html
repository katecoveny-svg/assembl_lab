Korimako

Bellbird · Anthornis melanura

An olive-green honeyeater with wine-red eyes and a ringing song that changes from one place to the next.

Where it lives

  • Native and exotic forest, scrub, farm shelter belts, urban parks and gardens on the North, South, Stewart and Auckland Islands and many offshore islands. NZ Birds Online
  • Notably rare on the mainland from Waikato northwards, except on the Coromandel Peninsula. Found from the coast up to about 1,200 metres. NZ Birds OnlineDOC

What it eats

  • Mainly nectar, from many native and introduced plants. NZ Birds Online
  • Also fruit in late summer and autumn, honeydew from scale insects on beech trunks, and insects and spiders. Invertebrates can make up to 85% of a bellbird’s energy intake. NZ Birds OnlineTe Ara

Notable adaptations

How its body and behaviour fit the way it lives.

  • A short, curved bill and a brush-like tongue reach nectar deep in flowers. NZ Birds Online
  • Tūī and bellbirds are important pollinators of plants including kōwhai, pūriri, tree fuchsia, flax and kākā beak. Te Ara
  • Males’ outer primary feathers are notched and whirr in flight. Males make the most of the sound in territorial disputes. NZ Birds Online
  • Young birds learn songs from their neighbours, so the tunes vary from place to place, forming a “local dialect”. Te Ara

Names and culture

Only what the sources say, in their words where it matters. To learn the names and stories of your own area, ask your local hapū and iwi.

  • Te Ara records that Māori would compare a person’s fine singing or oratory to the korimako, as a compliment. Te Ara

About this model

  • A stylised procedural reconstruction, not a scan or a measured specimen. Code builds every feather, shaped by eye from field-guide descriptions and photographs.
  • All eight birds are drawn at the same size on screen. In life the korimako is about 20 cm long. NZ Birds Online See all eight to scale.
  • Every model uses one shared wing shape and the same set of movements (breathing, looking round, hopping, preening). Don’t read wing shape or behaviour from the model.
  • Shows an adult male. Females are browner, with a narrow white-yellow cheek stripe.
  • The kōwhai sprig is stylised too, and not botanically checked.
